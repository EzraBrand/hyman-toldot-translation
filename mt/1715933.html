For the most part he is called in the Bavli by the name Resh Lakish, and in the Yerushalmi by the name R' Shimon ben Lakish, though in [Yerushalmi] Sanhedrin he is called in several places by the name Resh Lakish, and also in a few other places. Regarding this great man among giants, of whom it was said in Yoma 9b, "Anyone with whom Resh Lakish conversed in the marketplace, they would give him merchandise [on credit] without witnesses," a great confusion has arisen. There are those who wish to say that there were two Resh Lakishes, as brought in Torah MiTzion, Year 4, Booklet 4, page 28a, by the scholar R' S. David: that one was a contemporary of Rabbenu ha-Kadosh, and perhaps was even much older than Rabbi [Yehuda HaNasi], and the second was the brother-in-law of R' Yoḥanan. The sage already said, "In order to correct one error, we must commit many errors"; however, the wind shall carry away all his words. His difficulty from Bereishit Rabbah 78, that at the time of Rabbi's illness Resh Lakish went to inquire after his welfare and Rabbi requested of him to pray for him, and likewise there at the beginning of chapter 80, that Yosi of Ma'on slighted the honor of the Nasi, who is Rabbi, who was very angry, and in the evening Resh Lakish went to appease Rabbi—with all due respect, he made a great mistake in thinking that this "Rabbi" is Rabbenu ha-Kadosh, and did not know that all the Nesi'im were called in the Yerushalmi and in the midrashim (which copied the majority of their words from the Yerushalmi) by the name "Rabbi." And this "Rabbi" who requested of Resh Lakish to pray for him is the grandson of the grandson of Rabbi [Yehuda HaNasi], namely R' Yehuda Nesi'ah the Second, of whom Resh Lakish himself was at first not among his friends, as is explained in his entry, and afterward became one of his faithful friends. In my opinion, it is not worthwhile to deal any further with his words, as they have no foundation whatsoever.

And there are those who wish to say that he lived even before Ben Azzai, based on what is brought in Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer, chapter 43, that Ben Azzai said: "Come and see the power of repentance from R' Shimon ben Lakish, for he and his two companions were robbers and brigands—and he repented—and he engaged in Torah all his days—and on the day he died, his two robber companions died, and they gave to R' Shimon ben Lakish a portion in life, and to his two companions in the lowest netherworld." However, the Gaon R' David Luria, in his commentary on Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer, has already rightly pointed out that a scribal error fell into the texts regarding the name of Ben Azzai.

ויש שרוצים לומר שהיה עוד קודם בן עזאי ע״פ מה שמובא בפדר״א פמ״ג שאמר בן עזאי בא וראה כח התשובה מרשב״ל שהיה הוא ושני רעיו גוזלים וחומסים — והוא שב בתשובה — והיה עוסק בתורה כל ימיו — וביום שמת מתו שני רעיו השודדים ונתנו לרשב״ל חלק החיים ולשני רעיו בשאול תחתיות, אבל כבר העיר בצדק הגאון רד״ל בפירושו על הפדר״א שט"ס נפל בספרים בשם בן עזאי.

However, the clear truth is that there was one and only one R' Shimon ben Lakish, as we shall explain.

אבל האמת הברור שאך ורק הד ר׳ שמעון בן לקיש הוה וכאשר נבאר.

Whether the name Lakish is the name of his father or the name of a city, as is the opinion of Rabbi R. A. H. Weiss in Beit Talmud, Year 3, is unknown to us, since a city by the name of Lakish is not found anywhere. And as for why they shortened his name and called him by the name Resh Lakish, the Gaon R' Yeshayah Pik already remarked on this, and his opinion is that due to the fault of the early printers it was changed from R' Shimon ben Lakish to Resh Lakish. Yet it is difficult to say so and to declare every place where he is called by the name Resh Lakish to be an error; and likewise in Dikdukei Soferim he is brought in all the manuscripts several times by the name Resh Lakish. However, the true reason why they shortened his name is hidden from us; only this is known to us, that he called himself by the name "Bar Lakisha" in Megillah 28b, and so R' Yoḥanan called him in Bava Metzia 84a.

השם לקיש אם הוא שם אביו או שהוא שם עיר כדעת הרב ראה״וו כבית תלמוד שנה ג' לא נודע לנו, יען שלא נמצא בשום מקום עיר בשם לקיש, ומה שקיצרו את שמו וקראוהו בשם ריש לקיש כבר העיר ע״ז הגאון ר״י פיק ודעתו שהוא באשמת המדפיסים הראשונים נשתנה מרשב״ל לריש לקיש, וקשה לומר כן ולשבש בכל המקומות שנקרא בשם ר״ל, וכן מובא בדק״ס בכל הכ״י כמה פעמים בשם ריש לקיש, אבל טעם האמיתי נעלם ממנו למה קצרו שמו, ואך זאת ידוע לנו שקרא את עצמו בשם ״בר לקישא״ במגילה כח: וכן קראו ר׳ יוחנן כב״מ פד.

It is known to us that his ancestors were among the great men of the generation, as in Shabbat 119b, where Resh Lakish said to R' Yehuda Nesi'ah [II]: "Thus I have a tradition from my ancestors"—and some say, "from your ancestors"—"that any city in which there are no school-children, they destroy it."

אבותיו ידוע לנו שהיו מגדולי הדור כשבת קיט: שא״ל ר״ל לר' יהודה נשיאה ב) ״כך מקובלני מאבותי״ ואמרי לה מאבותיך כל עיר שאין בה תינוקות של בית רבן מחריבין אותה.

Now, the opinion of Rabbenu Tam in Tosafot Bava Metzia 84a, s.v. Iy hadart, is that initially Resh Lakish was a Torah scholar, and afterward he cast off the yoke of Torah from himself and became an ignoramus; and so they wrote in Eruvin 65b that Resh Lakish was a great man beforehand. With all due forgiveness from their holy bones, how is it possible to say such a thing about a holy one of Israel? Furthermore, from the very place that they brought proof

for their words, from there the exact opposite is proven! For they said there in Bava Metzia 84a: "He taught him Scripture and taught him Mishnah." How is it possible to say that if he had previously been a great man, he would forget even how to read the Torah, or even forget the alef-bet? And from Eruvin 65b, where they said: "Resh Lakish and the disciples of R' Ḥanina happened to come [to a certain place]... Resh Lakish said to them: 'Let us rent [now], and when we reach our masters in the South we shall ask them.' They came and asked R' Efes"—and they said there in Tosafot that R' Efes lived after Rabbi [Yehuda HaNasi] only two and a half years; if so, immediately after the passing of Rabbi, Resh Lakish was already the great man of the generation! And in the days of R' Ḥanina we find in Yerushalmi Sanhedrin 1:2: "R' Ḥanina opened [the discourse], R' Yoḥanan and Resh Lakish concluded." And likewise in [Yerushalmi] Niddah, at the end of chapter 2: "R' Ḥanina was residing in Tzippori, and R' Yoḥanan and Resh Lakish were residing there, and he did not join them to himself... One time he joined them to himself; they said, 'What did Rabbi see to take notice of us?' etc." And in the time of R' Yehuda Nesi'ah the First and R' Oshaya, we find in Yevamot 57a that R' Yoḥanan asked a question of R' Oshaya, and he said nothing to him; "Finally a great man came—and who was it? Resh Lakish—and resolved it for him. R' Yehuda Nesi'ah said to R' Oshaya: 'Is R' Yoḥanan not a great man?' etc." And this was even before R' Yoḥanan reigned [as head of the academy] in Tiberias, and they were all in Tzippori, and R' Efes and afterward R' Ḥanina were among the heads of the yeshiva there, and Resh Lakish and R' Yoḥanan were among their great disciples. If so, how could it enter the mind to say that suddenly Resh Lakish would abandon his way and his Torah, to cleave to brigands and robbers and to forget all his Torah? Rather, the truth shows its own way: that he was never a great man before the incident with R' Yoḥanan occurred, and he was then still in the very prime of his youth, as we shall explain. As it appears, he was born in Tzippori, the place of Rabbenu ha-Kadosh, and still merited to see him, as in Yerushalmi Beitzah 5:2: "R' Yoḥanan and R' Shimon ben Lakish said: 'We did not merit Torah except because we saw the fingers of Rabbi [protruding] from his boots.'" However, R' Yoḥanan was older than Resh Lakish, for R' Yoḥanan merited to be among the youngest disciples of Rabbi and heard halakhot from him, whereas Resh Lakish was a child and merited only to see him. And this is what we find in Ḥullin 54a, where Resh Lakish asked R' Yoḥanan: "'Who is Rav, and who is Rav, that I do not know him?' R' Yoḥanan said to him: 'Do you not remember that disciple who attended upon Rabbi the Great and R' Ḥiyya?'" And this is as we have said: that R' Yoḥanan, who at the passing of Rabbi was about fifteen years old, as is explained in his entry, therefore knew Rav and his greatness in the eyes of Rabbi well; whereas Resh Lakish, although he was the son of a great family in Tzippori and merited to see Rabbi, nevertheless did not know Rav and his greatness. And therefore we do not find in any place that Resh Lakish says [a teaching] in the name of Rabbi. And this child was endowed by nature with wondrous strength, so that he was not afraid of anyone, and he joined with men of valor like himself and became a leader over them, and strayed from the straight path and did not set his heart on the Torah, even though his ancestors were great men of the generation. Memories of his deeds in those days have remained for us in what is recounted in Gittin 47a, that he sold himself to the Ludim [gladiators] and relied on his strength to eradicate wickedness from the midst of the land—the sect of those who eat human flesh—and as is recounted there, he put them all to death. And this was always a common saying in his mouth, to say: "If you sold yourself to the Ludim..." as in Yerushalmi Terumot 8:3. And likewise, even when he was already among the foremost great men of the generation, it is recounted of him in Yerushalmi Terumot, at the end of chapter 8, that robbers seized R' Imi, until R' Yonatan despaired of [saving] him; however, R' Shimon ben Lakish said: "I will pursue them, and I will kill them or I will be killed," and the end was that he rescued him from their hands. And likewise it is recounted there that once bandits robbed R' Yoḥanan of all his money, and Resh Lakish pursued them, overtook them, and restored all his property. And R' Yoḥanan, who knew Resh Lakish well from his youth, and saw him how little by little he turned aside from the straight path and associated with wicked men, yearned greatly to bring him back to the good. Resh Lakish was then already in the very prime of his youth, and behold, God brought [an opportunity] into his hand,

With all his greatness and glory, he did not derive benefit from the honor of the Torah, but supported himself from his own property, and he himself guarded his orchards, as in Moed Katan 17a: "Resh Lakish was guarding an orchard. A certain man came and was eating figs. He shouted at him, and he paid no attention to him, so he excommunicated him. That man said to him: 'On the contrary, let that man (Resh Lakish) be under excommunication! Even if I have become liable to you for money, have I become liable to you for excommunication?' He came to the study hall, and they said to him: 'His is a valid excommunication; yours is not a valid excommunication,'" and he had to go to the house of the Patriarch so that they would annul it for him. And in Dor Dor ve-Dorshav, Part 3, page 72, he says: "After all the greatness of Resh Lakish, he did not merit to sit as head. However, there is no wonder in this — for in one matter Resh Lakish opposed the majority of the sages of his generation, and that was regarding the examination of the external mishnayot [beraitot] — and he established a rule and publicized it: 'Any teaching that was not taught in the academy, we do not rely upon it' (Yerushalmi Eruvin 1:6). And not only did he act according to this rule with the average sages of the generation, but even to R' Yoḥanan he did not show favor, and he refused to accept a beraita from him, saying: 'We have no need of this, for R' Yehuda HaNasi taught us a complete mishnah' (Yerushalmi Kilayim 1:6; Yerushalmi Bava Kamma, end of chapter 5) — and now the matter will be understood why Resh Lakish, with all his wisdom and virtues, did not succeed in rising to sit as head, and sat until the day of his death before R' Yoḥanan." Thus far his words. Now, as for the eulogy he makes over Resh Lakish and his bad fortune, his heart aches in vain. For without doubt, had Resh Lakish wanted to separate from his brother-in-law and beloved teacher, he would then have gone to another city and established a great yeshiva there, since the Sanhedrin had already been abolished from Israel and only houses of Torah took their place. And inasmuch as we see that Resh Lakish sat all his days with R' Yoḥanan, it is a sign of the preciousness of his heart, that his entire aim and desire was that the center of Torah should be in one place. And as is known, R' Yoḥanan was not only the head of the generation in Eretz Yisrael, but also in Babylonia his words were heeded like the words of the Urim after the passing of Rav and Shmuel. And Resh Lakish merited this good name just like R' Yoḥanan, for the halakhot of both of them were brought equally to the yeshivot of Babylonia. And had he lived after R' Yoḥanan, then without doubt Resh Lakish would have been the head of the yeshiva after him. However, because in his youth R' Yoḥanan was the primary teacher of Resh Lakish and was also older than him, therefore R' Yoḥanan reigned, and Resh Lakish could not reach the level of R' Yoḥanan, for "there is one leader for a generation." However, we have already explained above that Resh Lakish was like the right hand of R' Yoḥanan, and sometimes also stood in place of the head of the yeshiva; therefore we need not be distressed over this, for Resh Lakish too was never distressed in his days. However, regarding what the scholar Weiss wrote, that he also did not show favor to R' Yoḥanan and this was his sin for which he did not succeed in rising and sitting as head — begging his honor's pardon, he erred in the simple meaning of the text in Yerushalmi Kilayim and Bava Kamma there (which is a single passage). And this is the wording of the Yerushalmi there: "The wolf and the dog... although they resemble one another, they are kilayim [forbidden mixtures] with one another." And the Gemara there asks: "Have we not taught [this regarding] a bird? R' Yoḥanan said: 'I brought it from that of Bar Dalia,' (who said): 'The rooster with the pheasant and with the peacock, although they resemble one another, they are kilayim with one another.'" And regarding this Resh Lakish came and said: "R' Yehuda HaNasi taught a complete mishnah: 'And similarly beasts and birds that are like them.'" And so it is literally in Bava Kamma 55a, and there only the words of Resh Lakish are brought on the Mishnah, who said: "Here R' Yehuda HaNasi taught that the rooster, peacock, and pheasant are kilayim with one another." And it is a simple matter to any student that at first glance R' Yoḥanan did not notice that R' Yehuda HaNasi had already stated it anonymously in the Mishnah, and therefore he wanted to prove the law from the beraita of Bar Dalia, and Resh Lakish stood him upon the truth that we do not need this because the law was already taught in the Mishnah. And this is literally like what Ilfa said in Taanit 21a, that for all the beraitot of R' Ḥiyya and R' Oshaia he could show their source in the Mishnah of R' Yehuda HaNasi. And R' Yoḥanan said literally thus to R' Yannai his teacher in Makkot 21b: when R' Yannai said, "One who covers himself with kilayim is lashed," R' Yoḥanan said to him, "Is this not our Mishnah?" And similarly we find this expression throughout the Talmud; and if so, what refusal is there in the matter? Behold, he only stood him upon the truth. And as for what the aforementioned scholar said, that he established the rule and publicized it: "Any teaching that was not taught in the academy, we do not rely upon it" — this scholar has made us laugh! Behold, a rule greater than this is already known and famous, namely what they said in Ḥullin 141a, that any beraita that was not taught in the school of R' Ḥiyya and R' Oshaia is corrupted. And sound common sense dictates that if some student came to the study hall and brought a beraita in his hand, they did not accept it from him until they investigated and inquired whether one may rely upon him and whether it is not corrupted in his hand. Furthermore, he did not see what is explicit in Yerushalmi Gittin 3:1: "R' Yosi bar R' Bun said: It is not that Resh Lakish was contentious against R' Yoḥanan; rather... when he heard a beraita he relied upon it, and when he did not hear a beraita he nullified his own opinion before the opinion of R' Yoḥanan." And when his wife, the sister of R' Yoḥanan, died, he then married a wife in his old age, and she bore him sons, and therefore when Resh Lakish passed away he indeed left behind young sons. And only a small error fell into Bava Metzia 84a, and instead of "his sister [aḥateih] came" one must read "his wife [itteteih] came," and this is a small error in a manuscript between [the letters] ḥet and tav. And even though it is against the opinion of our master Rashi, who explains there that she was the sister of R' Yoḥanan, nevertheless it is known that our early masters were not precise in this, or that it is from some student who thought that the two stories there are one. And a support for this I found in Dikdukei Soferim, which brings from a manuscript: "his wife [deveit'hu] came." And they said in Gittin 47a: When Resh Lakish passed away, he left a kav of saffron, and he applied to himself [the verse]: "And leave their wealth to others" [Psalms 49:11]. And according to what we have said, Resh Lakish lived a long life of approximately more than ninety years, and R' Yoḥanan did not find his replacement until he weakened and passed away through this.
